# What Synchronet is

**Outcome: you can say what a BBS is, what
Synchronet adds to the idea, and what being
its sysop commits you to — before you
install anything.**

Check: if you can finish the sentence "a
caller connects to ___ and the sysop is
responsible for ___", you have this
chapter.

A **bulletin board system** is a computer
that hosts a small online community in
text. Callers connect to it directly, one
at a time or a few at once, and inside it
they find message boards organised by
topic, private mail between callers, files
to download, and sometimes games and live
chat. Nothing is published to the whole
internet; everything lives on that one
machine, under one person's control. In
the 1980s and 1990s callers arrived by
dial-up modem. Today they arrive over the
internet, but the shape is unchanged: a
door, a logon, a set of rooms.

**Synchronet** is a BBS package — the
software that turns a computer into that
host. It is free and open source, written
and still maintained by its original
author, and it is a modern program, not a
museum piece: the version this guide
installs announces support for UTF-8 and
classic terminal graphics, telnet, SSH and
rlogin connections, internet e-mail, FTP,
a web server, and a shelf of other
services, all in one program. What it
preserves from the dial-up era is the
*experience*: full-screen text menus,
one-key commands, ANSI artwork, and a
community small enough to know by name.

Three roles are worth fixing in your head:

- **The sysop** (system operator) — you.
  You install the software, name the
  board, decide which services run, and
  create the first account, which the
  board gives special powers. The sysop
  account is account number 1, and the
  board also guards it with a separate
  **system password** you will meet in
  chapter 5.
- **The callers** — everyone who logs on.
  Each caller registers with a name, a
  handle, and a password, answers a short
  questionnaire, and gets an account with
  an account number. Callers are also
  called *users*; the words mean the same
  thing here.
- **The board** — the whole installation
  as callers experience it: its name, its
  menus, its message boards, its rules.
  One Synchronet install is one board.

And the ethics line, early, because this
guide's standard insists on it: **the
sysop can read everything.** Messages in
every board, mail between callers — the
software assumes the operator can see it
all, because the operator is who callers
appeal to when something goes wrong. That
power arrives with the first account, not
when you feel ready for it. The honest
way to run a board is to say so to your
callers, plainly, and to treat their words
as theirs. Chapter 4 also keeps your first
board reachable only from your own
machine, so you can learn all of this with
nobody else's words at stake.

What a BBS is *for*, in a world of forums
and group chats, is a fair question, and
chapter 12 takes it seriously. The short
answer: a BBS is a place with a door. It
is small on purpose, it belongs to a
person rather than a platform, and its
history stays where it was written. Clubs,
ham radio groups, retro-computing circles
and households still run them for exactly
those reasons — and because logging on to
one is, frankly, fun in a way a feed is
not.
